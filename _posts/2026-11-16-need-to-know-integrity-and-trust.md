---
title: "The Honor of Knowing When Not to Share"
date: 2026-11-16 09:00:00 -0400
categories: [Essays]
tags: [ethics, integrity]
---

Information entrusted to us is not automatically ours to distribute. It may concern another person's safety, a company's unfinished research, a government's defensive capabilities, or a decision whose consequences extend far beyond the people discussing it. Receiving such information creates an obligation: to understand why it was shared, protect it appropriately, and resist the temptation to convert access into attention, influence, or personal advantage. A society that forgets this obligation makes trust increasingly expensive and cooperation increasingly fragile.

“Need to know” expresses that obligation in practical terms. In the United States national security system, Confidential, Secret, and Top Secret are classifications of information based on the expected damage from unauthorized disclosure. “Common” is not a formal classification, and unclassified does not necessarily mean publicly releasable. Clearance alone does not establish a right to every secret: access also requires a need for the particular information to perform an authorized function. Classification describes sensitivity; need to know connects access to responsibility. [^1]

The principle reaches beyond government. A colleague can be competent, honorable, and valued without needing access to every personnel matter or commercial negotiation. A person denied access has not necessarily been judged untrustworthy. The boundary may simply reflect a responsibility that belongs elsewhere. Recovering this distinction would relieve much of the resentment that turns ordinary discretion into an apparent insult.

Sometimes withholding information is the only responsible course, even when those seeking it feel entitled to know. Explaining every detail of a restriction can itself expose what the restriction protects. A custodian may therefore have to endure suspicion, criticism, or accusations of excessive secrecy while maintaining a justified boundary. A felt entitlement to transparency does not automatically establish a right to another person's confidence or to information whose disclosure could endanger others. Legitimate disclosure obligations and authorized oversight still apply; public pressure alone cannot settle the decision.

That recovery requires humility. Curiosity is not entitlement, proximity is not authorization, and possession is not understanding. A fragment of a strategy discussion can look reckless when separated from the alternatives being considered. A preliminary research result can look definitive when detached from its limitations. A headline can announce a controversy without supplying the evidence needed to judge it. The responsible reader recognizes these gaps; the responsible recipient does not exploit them.

It is tempting to describe our predicament as a fall from an earlier age of honor. History offers less reassurance. Robert Hanssen, an FBI agent who spied for the Soviet Union and Russia, received a life sentence in 2002. The Justice Department's inspector general found that the FBI's security programs had presented few obstacles to his espionage. Betrayal and institutional complacency were working together long before today's sharing platforms. [^2]; [^3]

Digitization changes the scale and speed of the problem. A person can copy a collection of documents, forward a conversation, or distribute a screenshot with little effort. Search makes scattered information easier to assemble; duplication makes recall difficult. The same conveniences that support productive collaboration can turn a moment of carelessness into an enduring exposure. Security therefore cannot depend on a promise made at hiring and remembered only after a breach.

A blog post, social network chat, or online article can carry that exposure far beyond its original audience. A remark intended for a small group can become a screenshot; the screenshot can become an accusation; the accusation can become a viral story. With each retelling, qualifications may disappear while certainty grows. The story acquires a life of its own, and a later correction cannot be assumed to reach everyone who encountered the original claim. The ability to publish instantly makes the discipline of pausing before publication more necessary.

That pause should be substantive. What is the full context? What remains unknown? Is the material authentic, complete, and authorized for this audience? Who could be harmed by its release or misinterpretation? Does the proposed disclosure help someone address a real problem, or merely invite spectators to pronounce judgment? When the full context is unavailable, responsible judgment includes acknowledging that limitation and withholding conclusions the evidence cannot support. Everyone who forwards the material inherits a share of this responsibility.

Information security also cannot depend on an idealized account of human nature. People are capable of generosity and courage, but also of greed, resentment, vanity, and self-deception. Someone may disclose information for money, another for recognition, and another from sincere conviction that a favored cause justifies the act. Sincerity does not eliminate consequences. A person can believe an action righteous while exposing people who never consented to bear its risks.

Pride leads to irresponsible behavior when it persuades us that our understanding is complete, our motives beyond question, and our judgment sufficient to override obligations to others. It can make restraint feel like weakness and access feel like something we are owed. Humility makes room for the possibility that we lack essential context or have mistaken personal conviction for moral authority. Integrity requires us to accept limits even when we could gain attention or approval by ignoring them.

Yet the same scrutiny must apply to those demanding secrecy. Executives and officials also have interests to protect, reputations to preserve, and incentives to rationalize misconduct. A credible ethic of confidentiality must bind the person who controls information as firmly as the person who receives it. Otherwise, appeals to honor become demands for obedience, and distrust becomes an understandable response.

Another confusion arises when we attribute a human personality, conscience, or unified political will to a company. Corporate legal personality has concrete purposes: a corporation can hold rights and obligations and act as a legal entity distinct from its members. That legal status should not be confused with the personal convictions of every employee, owner, or customer. [^4] Saying that a company “believes” something can conceal the more important questions of who adopted the position, under what authority, and with whose interests in view.

This conflation can become toxic when a leader's personal convictions acquire the force of institutional policy without adequate justification or review. In human resources, it can turn disagreement over a political or social question into an apparent test of professional loyalty. In public affairs, it can encourage people to treat a company's policy advocacy as though it expressed the views of everyone associated with it, or carried the democratic mandate of a public institution. The reverse error is equally damaging: treating an employee's personal statement as the position of the employer, or assigning personal guilt to everyone who works for an organization. These habits spread division while making responsibility harder to locate.

Human resources deserves explicit attention because its decisions shape hiring, advancement, training, workplace expectations, and the allocation of employees' time. HR can add to a company's mission—or dilute it—when programs introduce social or political objectives that have not been deliberately authorized through appropriate governance. The concern is mission expansion through routine administration, where employees may experience a new institutional commitment as a condition of belonging. HR should support the organization's stated purpose, lawful obligations, fair treatment, and employees' ability to do their work. Proposed additions should receive an explicit decision about their purpose, cost, authority, and effect on the core mission. Personal agreement with a favored cause should not become an informal qualification for employment or advancement.

A company can choose to spend money addressing social needs while declining to endorse a particular political movement or contested social or moral position. The approach advocated here is to support clearly defined services—such as emergency food, disaster recovery, or practical assistance to people in immediate need—through carefully evaluated programs whose work does not require recipients or employees to affirm an ideology. Funding a specific service should not be presented as blanket endorsement of every position held by the organization delivering it. No charitable category guarantees freedom from controversy, so recipient selection, funding restrictions, and communications must make the purpose clear. Employee participation should be voluntary, and the company should assess results rather than treat association with a cause as an achievement in itself.

Government and international assistance require the same discipline on a larger scale. When officials blur an authorized public mission with their own preferred causes, worthy language can discourage scrutiny of budgets, recipients, and results. That creates opportunities for mission drift, waste, and fraud. Claims of good intentions should increase the obligation to demonstrate that resources reach their intended purpose; they should never excuse weak verification or prevent scrutiny of a politically favored program.

The scale of documented losses makes this more than an abstract concern. In 2024, the Government Accountability Office estimated annual federal fraud losses of $233 billion to $521 billion using data from fiscal years 2018–2022. These are estimates across differing risk environments, not a tally of adjudicated cases or a finding that all losses arose from ideological spending. [^5] In international reconstruction, the Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction reported in 2021 that roughly $2.4 billion of nearly $7.8 billion in reviewed U.S.-funded capital assets were unused or abandoned, used for unintended purposes, deteriorated, or destroyed. [^6]

Those findings establish serious failures of stewardship; they do not by themselves establish that conflating personal causes with public missions caused the losses. Fraud, improper payments, and waste also require distinct findings: an error or failed project is not automatically intentional deception. The practical lesson is to test every program against its authorized purpose, actual beneficiaries, verified expenditures, and demonstrated outcomes. Domestic and global projects alike need independent review, enforceable funding conditions, and the willingness to stop, redesign, or investigate work that fails those tests. Protecting a mission requires protecting it from misuse by people who invoke its moral importance.

Clear boundaries protect personal autonomy, corporate purpose, and governmental responsibility. Companies need authority to manage work and protect legitimate institutional interests; that authority should have a defined scope and an accountable basis. Public officials must exercise their own duties rather than substitute corporate influence or personal allegiance for public judgment. Individuals should be answerable for their conduct without having every private conviction absorbed into an employer's identity. In each setting, the practical question is who is acting, in what capacity, with what authority, and subject to what review.

Intellectual agreement with these distinctions is insufficient. They must be reflected in decision rights, personnel practices, communications rules, conflict disclosures, and enforceable consequences for misuse of authority. The same discipline applies to need to know: a leader's personal curiosity does not establish an institutional need for access, and an employee's private cause does not authorize disclosure of entrusted information. Nor should the phrase “the company decided” obscure the people responsible for a decision or erase the organization's own accountability. Integrity requires both identifiable responsibility and boundaries that hold when influential people would prefer an exception.

Recent disputes at OpenAI illustrate why judgments require care. In October 2026, the Associated Press reported that OpenAI fired three safety researchers, citing violations of sensitive-information policies. The researchers disputed the company's explanation and said safety work was at issue. The competing accounts should remain visible when discussing the case. [^7]

An earlier episode received similar attention: in April 2024, *The Information* reported that OpenAI dismissed two researchers for alleged leaks; what was disclosed was unclear. [^8] One researcher subsequently disputed the basis for his dismissal, according to *TIME*. [^9]

These cases illustrate the tension between confidentiality and accountability; they do not, by themselves, establish betrayal, greed, retaliation, or the merits of unrelated litigation. Calling someone disloyal without sufficient evidence repeats the very failure of judgment that responsible information handling should prevent. Likewise, allegations involving injury or mental health require serious examination. Neither a company's assurances nor a complainant's accusations should substitute for evidence.

Transparency needs similar precision. Public accountability does not require publishing every operational detail. An independent reviewer can examine sensitive records while the public receives findings, reasons, and an account of corrective action. Conversely, an institution that publishes abundant material while concealing decisive evidence may be highly visible and poorly accountable. What matters is whether people with legitimate oversight responsibilities can discover the truth and act on it.

Failure to impose consequences on people who abuse trusted positions can make demands for unlimited transparency seem justified. When insiders appear insulated from discipline, the public has reason to doubt assurances that existing safeguards work. But exposing everything does not necessarily correct that failure. It can leave the misconduct unpunished while compromising information that protects uninvolved people, legitimate operations, or essential capabilities. The remedy must reach the abuse itself: establish the facts, impose proportionate consequences, repair failed controls, and provide credible evidence that corrective action occurred. Otherwise, the swing toward indiscriminate disclosure adds another injury to the original breach of trust.

The national security framework itself recognizes a boundary: Executive Order 13526 prohibits classification to conceal legal violations, inefficiency, or administrative error, or to prevent embarrassment. That limitation belongs beside the duty to safeguard information. [^10] Confidentiality deserves public confidence when its purpose can withstand independent examination.

For that examination to mean anything, people need credible places to adjudicate disputes. An institution cannot earn trust merely by declaring its own reviewers unbiased. Impartiality requires safeguards: disclosure of conflicts, recusal where appropriate, a fair opportunity to challenge evidence, protection for sensitive material, reasoned decisions, and meaningful review. These arrangements must allow the relevant context to be examined without forcing every confidential fact into public circulation. They must also give people somewhere to turn when the institution accused of wrongdoing controls the ordinary reporting channel.

The United States judiciary bears a particular responsibility here, but judicial office does not make its holder immune to political pressure or failures of judgment. The Code of Conduct for United States Judges expressly calls for independence and impartiality and rejects political influence on judicial conduct. Those standards matter precisely because public confidence cannot rest on titles alone. A controversial ruling is not, by itself, evidence of capitulation to politics; allegations of bias require the same attention to evidence that we demand elsewhere. [^11]

History nevertheless supplies concrete reasons to insist on correction as well as independence. In the Korematsu case, the courts sustained a conviction arising from the wartime exclusion of Japanese Americans. Decades later, evidence of government misconduct in presenting the claimed military necessity led a federal district court to vacate Fred Korematsu's conviction in 1983. The episode demonstrates both a grave failure of judicial protection and the possibility of judicial repair when the evidentiary record is confronted. [^12]

We should expect and support judges and other adjudicators who correct demonstrated errors, even when doing so is difficult, politically inconvenient, or unpopular. Correction should follow evidence and law, with reasons that can be examined. The courage to reverse an error is part of institutional integrity; so is the courage to uphold a sound decision against an angry audience. Neither duty can be fulfilled by treating public applause as a measure of justice.

“Trust but verify” gives this ethic operational force. Trust allows people to undertake meaningful responsibility. Verification establishes whether the conditions supporting that trust still hold. Reviews, access records, and separation of duties protect against deliberate abuse, but also against mistakes and compromised accounts. Their purpose should be explained in advance and applied consistently, including to senior leaders. A system that exempts powerful insiders teaches everyone else that security is a hierarchy of privilege.

In computing, NIST's zero trust architecture offers a related discipline: network location or ownership alone should not confer implicit trust, and access requires authentication and authorization. This is a technical principle, not a declaration that every colleague is morally suspect. Its broader lesson is that a familiar identity or an internal connection cannot settle every access decision. [^13]

Some risks warrant restrictions that feel draconian to people accustomed to immediate access: prohibiting personal devices in sensitive work areas, limiting copying, requiring a second authorized person for especially consequential actions, or keeping selected information on systems physically disconnected from networks. Such measures should have a documented purpose, accountable authorization, and periodic review. Their inconvenience does not by itself make them excessive. Where compromise could cause grave harm, accepting inconvenience may be part of the duty of care.

Keeping selected data offline can sometimes provide the strongest practical protection against remote network attacks by removing the connection those attacks would use. A concrete application is CISA's recommendation to maintain offline, encrypted backups of critical data and regularly test their availability and integrity. [^14] That recommendation concerns recovery; an offline backup does not prevent theft of an online working copy. For confidentiality, deciding which working data should remain disconnected requires its own risk assessment. Offline storage also needs physical safeguards, controlled transfers, and continuity arrangements: people, removable media, and later reconnection can still introduce risks. The point is to choose connectivity deliberately rather than assume every useful piece of information belongs on a connected system.

Several practical commitments can turn these principles into an institutional culture:

1. **Make restrictions intelligible and proportionate.** Define what needs protection, the harm disclosure could cause, who owns the decision, and when it will be reviewed. Companies should use clear business categories without implying that their labels confer government classification authority. Excessive restriction encourages workarounds and obscures the information that truly matters.

2. **Connect access to actual responsibilities.** Grant the information and capabilities needed for an assignment, revisit permissions when duties change, and remove obsolete access. Provide enough context for competent work, including awareness of hazards and obligations. Need to know should support responsible action rather than make it impossible.

3. **Partition knowledge with coordination in mind.** Separate especially sensitive components when doing so limits the consequences of compromise. Designate accountable people who can see how the components interact. Excessive compartmentalization can hide a dangerous dependency or prevent anyone from recognizing the whole problem.

4. **Preserve essential knowledge securely.** Keeping critical knowledge entirely unwritten may reduce one exposure while creating dependence on a person's memory or continued availability. Prefer protected documentation, limited access, and controlled succession. Decisions to avoid recording something should account for continuity, oversight, and applicable recordkeeping obligations; silence should never become a routine way to evade accountability.

5. **Verify conduct without making surveillance limitless.** Review sensitive access, investigate unusual activity, and maintain reliable audit records. Limit who can inspect those records and how long they are retained. Monitoring itself creates sensitive information and opportunities for abuse. Alerts should trigger fair inquiry, not automatic declarations of guilt.

6. **Provide credible channels for reporting wrongdoing.** People need routes to independent reviewers, appropriate regulators, or other authorized recipients when their ordinary management chain is implicated. Those routes must have authority, confidentiality safeguards, and protection against retaliation. For a concrete federal example, the Office of Special Counsel accepts disclosures of wrongdoing but expressly instructs filers not to submit classified material through its online portal; anyone needing such material to support a disclosure should discuss options with the assigned attorney. Safe reporting requires an appropriate channel, not indiscriminate distribution. [^15]

7. **Match consequences to evidence and conduct.** An accidental mistake, reckless handling, deliberate theft, and a good-faith report through an appropriate channel require different responses. Investigate intent, authorization, harm, and institutional failures. Enforce proportionate consequences for substantiated misconduct regardless of rank or political connections, and verify that promised remedies occur. Encourage prompt reporting of mistakes so that fear does not turn a containable incident into a concealed one.

8. **Make leaders demonstrate the discipline they require.** Officials and executives should honor restrictions when disclosure would help their own reputation or political position. Reward quiet reliability, accurate reporting, and willingness to admit uncertainty. Audit exceptions as carefully as ordinary compliance. Honor loses its meaning when it applies only to subordinates.

9. **Make careful sharing and fair review routine.** Establish a clear review path before sensitive material reaches blogs, social networks, or external publications. Teach people to check audience, authorization, context, and foreseeable consequences before posting or forwarding. Provide independent avenues to challenge restrictions and findings, and publish appropriately limited explanations of decisions. When an error has spread, correct it promptly through the channels that carried it and make the correction easy to find.

10. **Be willing to withhold access and disconnect systems.** Identify information whose exposure would justify unusually strict handling, including offline storage where appropriate. Give custodians clear authority to decline unauthorized requests, even from influential people. Test whether the restrictions actually reduce the relevant risks and whether essential work and authorized oversight can still proceed. Reconsider the controls when circumstances change, without making popularity the test of their legitimacy.

11. **Enforce the distinction between personal views and institutional authority.** Specify who may adopt policy, speak for the organization, authorize disclosures, and commit organizational resources to public advocacy. Require consequential decisions to identify their responsible owners, institutional purpose, and approval basis. Evaluate personnel decisions against documented job requirements and applicable protections, with independent review of alleged retaliation or ideological favoritism. Correct false attribution of personal views to institutions—or institutional positions to individuals—and apply proportionate consequences when people misuse their office. These boundaries should protect legitimate missions without shielding either individuals or organizations from responsibility.

12. **Keep HR programs and social spending accountable to mission.** Require an explicit mandate, budget, measurable purpose, and review date for programs that extend beyond core operations. Keep hiring and advancement tied to legitimate job requirements, and make charitable participation voluntary. For corporate giving, specify the service being funded and avoid implying endorsement of a recipient's unrelated advocacy. For public spending, verify statutory or other applicable authority, recipient eligibility, delivery, and results. Audit conflicts of interest and spending, enforce corrective actions, and discontinue or redesign programs that cannot justify their continuation.

The press and the public also have responsibilities. Before amplifying leaked material, an editor should weigh authenticity, context, public interest, and foreseeable harm. Publication can expose serious wrongdoing; it can also expose uninvolved people or sensitive capabilities without adding meaningful accountability. Readers should distinguish what a document proves from what a commentator claims it proves. Declining to pass along an unverified accusation is an exercise of judgment.

Political integrity demands that these standards survive a change of sides. A disclosure does not become responsible merely because it harms an opponent, and secrecy does not become legitimate merely because it protects an ally. The same questions should follow every case: What duty applied? What evidence supports the allegation? Who needed access? What harm was foreseeable? What responsible alternatives existed? Who can independently review the decision?

To love one's neighbor includes protecting confidences that safeguard that neighbor's life, work, and dignity. It also includes refusing to let confidentiality become a shelter for abuse. Both obligations require the courage to disappoint someone: the curious colleague, the impatient audience, the partisan ally, or the powerful superior.

An honorable information culture begins with people who can be trusted to speak truthfully, remain silent responsibly, and recognize when silence must give way to an accountable report. Institutions must make those choices practicable and verify that they are honored. The person receiving a confidence should ask what duty comes with knowing. The person imposing a restriction should be ready to justify it to legitimate oversight. Trust becomes durable when both accept that responsibility.
